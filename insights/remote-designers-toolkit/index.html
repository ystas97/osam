The Remote Designer's Toolkit

Tools don’t make a designer. A great designer with a pencil beats an average one with every subscription on the market. But on a remote team, tools do something else: they replace the office. They’re where you meet, share, argue, decide and hand work over.

By OSAM design team

Pick the right stack and a team spread across time zones feels like it’s sitting in one room. Pick the wrong one and you spend half the week asking where the latest file is.

At OSAM we design and build websites and iOS apps fully remotely, from first research to App Store release. Here’s the toolkit we think every remote designer should know, grouped by the job each tool does.

Design and prototyping: Figma at the center

For interface design, Figma is the shared table everyone sits around. It runs in the browser, several people can work in one file at once, and anyone with a link can see the latest version. For a remote team, that alone removes a whole category of problems.

What’s worth mastering, beyond drawing rectangles:

  • Components and variants. Build a button once, use it everywhere, change it in one place. This is what turns a pile of screens into a product.
  • Auto layout. Designs that resize like real interfaces do, which makes the jump to code far smoother.
  • Styles and variables. Colors, type and spacing defined once, so the whole team speaks the same visual language.
  • Prototyping. Clickable flows that let clients and users feel the product before a single line of code exists.
  • Comments. Feedback right on the design, tied to the exact element, instead of lost in a chat thread.

A well-organized Figma file is a form of communication. Clear page names, a cover, a section marked "ready for dev" and nobody needs to ask you which frame is final.

Visuals and motion: Adobe and friends

Figma owns the interface, but a lot of design lives outside it. That’s where Adobe Creative Suite still earns its place.

  • Photoshop for photo editing, retouching and the image work that makes a landing page or App Store screenshot look premium.
  • Illustrator for logos, icons and illustrations that need clean, scalable vectors.
  • After Effects for motion: launch videos, animated product demos and promo clips. A static screenshot shows a product. A ten-second video sells it.

You don’t need to be an expert in all of them. But a UI designer who can retouch a hero image or animate a logo reveal is far more useful on a small remote team than one who has to hand every non-Figma task to someone else.

Collaboration: replacing the office

This is the part of the stack most designers underestimate, and the part that matters most when you never share a room.

  • Whiteboards (FigJam, Miro). For research boards, user journeys, brainstorms and workshops with clients. The closest thing to standing at a wall with sticky notes.
  • Chat (Slack, Telegram, Teams). For quick questions and daily rhythm. The rule that saves everyone: one channel per project, so nothing gets buried.
  • Video calls (Zoom, Google Meet). For kickoffs, design reviews and the conversations that are faster said than typed.
  • Screen recordings (Loom and similar). The most underrated remote tool. A three-minute walkthrough of a design explains your thinking better than a page of text, and your teammate can watch it in their own time zone.
  • Docs and tasks (Notion, Linear, Jira, Trello). Where decisions, briefs and to-dos live, so the answer to "what did we agree on?" is always a link away.

Which exact app you use matters less than using it consistently. A team that agrees where things live beats a team with the best tools and no rules.

Handoff: from Figma to a real product

A design isn’t done when it looks good in Figma. It’s done when it looks the same in the user’s hands. Handoff is where many remote projects quietly lose quality, because the designer and developer can’t just lean over and point at a screen.

  • Figma’s developer handoff features let developers inspect spacing, colors, fonts and export assets without asking you for every number.
  • Xcode is where iOS apps are built. A designer doesn’t need to write Swift, but knowing how native iOS components behave helps you design screens that can be built pixel-perfect, not approximately.
  • Browser dev tools are worth knowing for web projects. Being able to check a live page against the design catches problems before the client does.

At OSAM, design and development live under one roof, and that’s not just a slogan. It’s the toolchain: what’s approved in Figma is what gets built in Xcode or the browser.

AI tools: the newest part of the kit

AI is now part of almost every designer’s toolkit, whether as a separate app or built into the tools above. Used well, it takes the boring parts off your plate.

  • Research. Summarizing interview notes, clustering feedback, drafting survey questions.
  • Exploration. Generating quick visual directions, mood references or layout ideas to react to.
  • Copy. Placeholder text that actually sounds like the product, button labels, App Store descriptions to refine.
  • Images. Background cleanup, extending photos, quick variations for ads and screenshots.

The catch: AI produces a lot of average very quickly. The tool can give you fifty options. Your job is knowing which one is right and why. Treat AI output as a first draft from an eager intern, never as the final answer.

How to build your stack without drowning in tools

There’s a new design tool every week, and every one of them promises to change your workflow forever. Most won’t.

  1. Master the core first. Get genuinely good at Figma before collecting plugins and side apps. Depth beats breadth.
  2. Add tools for problems, not for fun. Only adopt something new when you can name the exact problem it solves.
  3. Match the team. On a remote team, the best tool is the one everyone else already uses. Your favorite app is useless if nobody else opens it.
  4. Review once in a while. Every few months, drop what you don’t use. Fewer tabs, fewer subscriptions, more focus.

A lean, well-used stack makes you faster than a long list of tools you half know.
