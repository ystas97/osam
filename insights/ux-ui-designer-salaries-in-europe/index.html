UX/UI Designer Salaries in Europe

Ask ten designers what they earn and you’ll get ten different answers, three "it depends" and one awkward silence. Salary talk in design is confusing because the same title can mean very different jobs, and because Europe isn’t one market.

By OSAM design team

It’s dozens of markets, with different taxes, contracts and costs of living. This guide gives you a realistic picture of what UX/UI and product designers earn across Europe in 2026, by level and by country, plus what moves your number up and how to talk about it.

A quick note on the numbers: they’re indicative ranges compiled from public salary guides and surveys, shown as gross annual pay. Real offers vary by company, city and contract type, so treat them as a compass, not a price tag.

Junior, middle, senior: what the levels really mean

Years of experience are a rough guide, but companies pay for responsibility, not birthdays.

  • Junior (roughly 0 to 2 years). You design screens and components within an existing system, with guidance. Your job is to execute well and learn fast. Pay is at the bottom of the range, and the biggest jumps come from moving up, not from negotiating hard at this stage.
  • Middle (roughly 2 to 5 years). You own features end to end: research, flows, design and handoff, with little supervision. This is where most designers spend the longest, and where pay spreads the most depending on the company.
  • Senior (roughly 5+ years). You shape the product, not just the screens. You make calls on direction, mentor others, push back on bad ideas and connect design to business results. Across European markets, the step from middle to senior is often the biggest pay jump in a designer’s career.

A practical test: if you still need someone to tell you what to design next, you’re not senior yet, whatever your title says.

What designers earn, country by country

The spread is wide. A senior designer in Germany can earn roughly what a lead does in Spain, while Poland sits lower in euros but offers strong purchasing power locally. Here are indicative gross annual ranges for 2026:

Sources: Jobrise salary guides for Germany, Vienna and Spain. Freenance for Poland (Warsaw, employment contract, monthly PLN converted at roughly 4.25 PLN per EUR). A few more markets worth knowing:

  • United Kingdom. One recruiter projects the average UX/UI design salary in the UK at around £55,700 for 2026, with seniors well above that in London.
  • Serbia and the Western Balkans. Local salaries are lower in absolute terms. Senior UI roles in Belgrade are estimated at roughly €2,000 to €3,500 gross per month, while junior roles in the region often start around €1,300 gross per month.
  • Contract type matters. In Poland and much of Central and Eastern Europe, B2B contracts are common and usually pay more on paper than employment contracts, but without paid leave or social benefits. Always compare like with like.

Remote work: does location still decide your pay?

Remote work was supposed to flatten salaries across Europe. It didn’t, but it did blur the lines. Companies hiring remotely usually follow one of three models:

  • Location-based pay. Your salary follows the market where you live. A designer in Belgrade and one in Berlin get different offers for the same role.
  • Regional bands. One range for, say, Western Europe and another for Central and Eastern Europe. A middle ground that’s increasingly common.
  • One global rate. Same role, same pay, wherever you are. Rarer, and usually at companies that compete hard for talent.

For designers in lower-cost countries, remote work for international companies can mean a big jump compared with local offers. For designers in high-cost countries, it can mean competing with talent from everywhere. Remote roles across Europe are also often offered as contractor positions rather than employment, so check what’s included before comparing numbers.

One honest tip: ask early which model a company uses. It saves both sides from a mismatch three interviews in.

What moves your number up

Two designers with the same years of experience can be tens of thousands apart. Here’s usually why:

  • Product designer vs UI/UX designer. Roles that own research, strategy and business outcomes tend to pay more than roles focused mainly on visuals.
  • Industry. Fintech, healthtech and AI products typically pay a premium over agencies, media or e-commerce.
  • Shipped results. "I redesigned the onboarding and activation went up" is worth more than any title. Proof of impact is the strongest salary argument there is.
  • Range beyond screens. Designers who understand development, motion or App Store optimization are more valuable on small teams.
  • English and communication. For international and remote roles, being able to present and defend your work in English widens the pool of companies that can hire you.
  • AI fluency. Designers who use AI to move faster, without losing quality, can take on more and are increasingly sought after.
  • Your portfolio. A clear site with strong case studies changes the conversation before salary even comes up.

How to name your expectations

Knowing the market is half the job. The other half is saying a number out loud.

  • Name a real number. "Negotiable" or "depends on the project" leaves the company guessing. Say the amount you’d be genuinely happy to work for: not the minimum you’d survive on, not a number inflated just in case.
  • Be clear about the format. Gross or net, monthly or yearly, employment or contract, which currency. Half of all salary confusion is two people talking about different numbers.
  • Use a narrow range if you prefer. "€3,500 to €4,000 gross a month" works. A range wider than that just signals you haven’t decided.
  • Look at the whole package. Remote setup, paid time off, learning budget, flexible hours. Sometimes a slightly lower number with better conditions is the better deal.
  • Revisit it every year. Your market value changes as your skills and results grow. Your salary expectation should too.

Clear expectations don’t scare good companies away. They save everyone time and make the conversation about fit, not about decoding vague messages.
