Why Your Design Application Gets Skipped

We get more than 300 job applications a day at OSAM. Every single one is reviewed by a real person, by hand, with no AI filtering. That means we see a lot of talented designers. It also means we see the same few mistakes over and over again.

By OSAM design team

October 16, 20265 min read

Contents

  2. Mistake 2: A portfolio that’s badly presented
  3. Mistake 3: Weak work "because the client wanted it that way"

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: most applications don’t get rejected because the designer is bad. They get skipped because something in the first few seconds makes the reviewer’s job harder. When there are hundreds more waiting, nobody has time to decode what you meant. They just move on to the next one.

These are the four mistakes we see most often, and how to fix each of them.

Mistake 1: No portfolio at all

It sounds strange, but it happens every day. A designer applies for a design job and doesn’t attach a single example of their work. Sometimes there’s a resume. Sometimes there’s just "Hi, I’m interested, here’s my Telegram."

Think about it from our side. How are we supposed to choose a designer without seeing their design? Years of experience, a list of tools and a nice cover letter tell us almost nothing about how you actually work. Your portfolio is your work speaking for you, and without it, there’s simply nothing to evaluate.

The fix is simple: always attach your portfolio, and make sure the link works and opens without requesting access. An application without a portfolio isn’t an application yet.

Mistake 2: A portfolio that’s badly presented

You’re applying for a job where presentation is the job. So the way you present your own work is the first design test, whether you like it or not.

That’s why a Google Doc with screenshots pasted in a row doesn’t work. Neither does a folder of random files or a PDF that takes a minute to load. In today’s world it’s simply not acceptable for a designer.

Here’s the scale we use:

  • Minimum: a Behance profile with properly laid-out projects.
  • Better: your own website with your projects.

A personal site is the strongest option because it does two jobs at once. It shows your projects, and the site itself shows what you can do. Even if your client work isn’t the most impressive, a well-designed portfolio site proves your taste, your layout skills and your attention to detail before we’ve opened a single case study.

Mistake 3: Weak work "because the client wanted it that way"

We get it, honestly. This is one of the most common problems in the industry. The client insisted on five fonts, a neon gradient and their nephew’s logo. You did what was asked, the client was happy, and now that project sits in your portfolio looking nothing like what you’re capable of.

The problem is that we can only judge what we see. We can’t tell the difference between "this designer has weak taste" and "this designer had a difficult client." From the outside, both look exactly the same.

The fix: add concept projects. Pick a product you’d love to redesign or invent one of your own, and design it as well as you possibly can, with no client limiting you. Show us what you do when the only standard is yours.

Just label them clearly as concepts or personal projects. That’s completely normal and we value them, but presenting a concept as real client work is a red flag. Honesty is part of the portfolio too.

Mistake 4: No salary expectations

When the form asks for your salary expectations, many designers leave it blank or write "negotiable" or "depends on the project." We understand why. You’re afraid of asking too little and underselling yourself, or asking too much and scaring us off.

But vague answers don’t protect you. They just leave us guessing, and guessing slows everything down.

Our advice: name the number you’d be genuinely happy to work for. Not the lowest you could survive on, not a number inflated just in case. The amount that would let you do great work without feeling underpaid.

That honesty helps both sides. You’ll feel comfortable if we agree, and we’ll immediately understand what you’re expecting instead of trying to read between the lines of "we can discuss it." If the numbers don’t match, we’ll both know early. If they do, we can move straight to the interesting part.

The one rule behind all four

All of these mistakes come down to the same thing: they make the person reviewing your application work harder.

Picture the HR manager opening your message for the first time. They don’t know you, they have hundreds of other applications waiting, and they have a few seconds to decide whether to look closer. If they hit a missing portfolio, a messy Google Doc or a blank salary field, they won’t stop to investigate what you meant. They’ll simply move to the next application.

So before you hit send, check your application with fresh eyes:

  • Is the portfolio attached, and does the link open without asking for access?
  • Is it presented on Behance or your own site, not in a doc or a folder?
  • Does it show work you’re proud of, including concept projects if needed?
  • Have you named your salary expectations clearly?

Make your application as easy as possible to say yes to. That alone puts you ahead of most of the pile.
