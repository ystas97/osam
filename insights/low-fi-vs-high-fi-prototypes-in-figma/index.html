Low-Fi vs High-Fi Prototypes in Figma

Here’s a scene every designer knows. You spend a week polishing a gorgeous, pixel-perfect prototype. You show it to the client. And the first comment is: "Actually, I don’t think we need this screen at all."

By OSAM design team

That week didn’t go to waste because the design was bad. It went to waste because the fidelity was wrong for the question you were trying to answer.

Low-fidelity and high-fidelity prototypes aren’t steps of quality. They’re different tools for different jobs. Picking the right one at the right moment saves days of work and gets you better feedback. Here’s how to tell which one you need, and how to build each in Figma.

Low-fidelity: test the idea, not the pixels

A low-fidelity prototype is rough on purpose: grey boxes, placeholder text, simple shapes and basic links between screens. No colors, no real images, no polish. Use it when you need to answer:

  • Does this flow make sense at all?
  • What should be on this screen, and in what order?
  • Which of these three structures works best?
  • Are we solving the right problem?

Why it works: it’s fast to make and cheap to throw away. Even more important, people react to structure instead of style. Show a polished screen and you get comments about the shade of blue. Show grey boxes and you get comments about whether the flow works, which is exactly what you need early on.

How to build it in Figma:

  • Use a simple wireframe kit or your own grey components: boxes, lines, text blocks.
  • Keep everything in one or two greys so nobody mistakes it for a final design.
  • Link screens with basic click-through interactions. No animations needed.
  • Use FigJam or a whiteboard for even earlier flows and sketches.
  • Label it clearly as a wireframe when you share it with clients.

High-fidelity: test the experience

A high-fidelity prototype looks and behaves almost like the real product: final visuals, real content, actual components, transitions and interactive states. Use it when you need to answer:

  • Do users understand the details: labels, icons, hierarchy?
  • Does the interaction feel right: transitions, gestures, feedback?
  • Will stakeholders or investors buy into the vision?
  • Is everything ready for developers to build exactly as designed?

Why it works: it shows the product as users will actually experience it. Usability tests with high-fidelity prototypes reveal problems that grey boxes can’t, like confusing visual hierarchy or an icon nobody recognizes. And for handoff, it removes guesswork.

How to build it in Figma:

  • Build with real components and variables from your design system.
  • Use real or realistic content, not "Lorem ipsum." Real text exposes layout problems fast.
  • Add interactive components for states: pressed buttons, toggles, inputs with errors.
  • Use smart animate for key transitions and variables or conditional logic where a flow needs to remember choices.
  • Test it on a real phone with the Figma mobile app, not only on your monitor.

A simple rule: match fidelity to how sure you are. The less certain you are about the structure, the lower the fidelity should be. Once the flow is proven, invest in detail.

In practice, most projects move through both. In our own process, the first weeks go to research and concepts, where structure and direction get decided, and the polished design comes after the client has picked a direction. That order is what keeps a week of polish from landing on a screen nobody needed.

There’s also a middle ground. Mid-fidelity prototypes use real layout and typography but simple visuals. They’re great for testing content and hierarchy without committing to a visual style.

And AI is blurring the lines. Prompt-to-UI tools can now generate fairly polished screens in minutes. That makes it tempting to skip low-fi. Don’t: a good-looking generated screen still invites feedback on looks before the structure is right. Use AI to explore faster, but keep asking the low-fi questions first.

Common mistakes

  • Polishing too early. The classic. A beautiful prototype makes people reluctant to suggest big changes, even when the structure is wrong.
  • Staying rough too long. Testing details like icon meaning or visual hierarchy with grey boxes gives misleading results. At some point, users need to see the real thing.
  • Showing wireframes without context. Clients who’ve never seen a wireframe may think it’s the final design. Explain what they’re looking at and what kind of feedback you need.
  • Prototyping every screen. A prototype only needs the screens for the flow you’re testing. The settings page can wait.
  • Ignoring edge cases in high-fi. A polished happy path with no error, empty or loading states hides the hardest design work until development.
  • Fake content. "Lorem ipsum" and perfect stock photos hide real problems. Long names, missing images and three-line titles appear the moment real data arrives.
  • Never testing on a device. A prototype that works on a 27-inch monitor can feel cramped and awkward on a phone in one hand.
