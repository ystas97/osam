Whiteboard Challenge: How Not to Freeze

"Design a parking app. You have 45 minutes. Go." That’s the whiteboard challenge: a live design exercise during an interview where you solve a problem in real time while the interviewers watch.

By OSAM design team

No Figma polish, no time to hide mistakes, just you, a board and a problem you’ve never seen before. It’s one of the most stressful parts of a product designer’s interview, mostly because people misunderstand what it tests.

Here’s the secret: nobody expects a finished design in 45 minutes. The whiteboard challenge isn’t about the answer. It’s about how you get there.

What interviewers are really watching

A portfolio shows your results. A whiteboard shows your thinking, live and unedited. That’s why companies use it. While you work, interviewers are quietly checking a few things:

  • Do you ask before you draw? Great designers clarify the problem first. Weak ones jump straight into screens.
  • Do you think about people? Who is the user, what do they need, in what situation will they use this?
  • Can you structure chaos? Breaking a fuzzy prompt into clear steps is the core skill of product design.
  • Can you prioritize? You can’t solve everything in 45 minutes. Choosing what matters most is the point.
  • Can you explain yourself? Talking through your reasoning is as important as the sketches.
  • How do you handle feedback? When an interviewer pushes back, do you get defensive or get curious?

Notice what’s missing from that list: pretty visuals. A rough sketch with sharp reasoning beats a beautiful screen with no explanation every time.

A simple framework for the session

Having a structure in your head is the best cure for freezing. For a typical 45 to 60 minute challenge, this flow works well:

  1. Clarify the problem (5 to 10 minutes). Ask questions. Who is this for? What’s the business goal? What platform? Are there constraints? Write the answers on the board so everyone sees them.
  2. Define the user and the scenario. Pick one main user and one key situation. "A driver arriving in an unfamiliar city center, in a hurry, one hand on the wheel" gives you something real to design for.
  3. Map the journey. Sketch the steps from the user’s goal to the result. Spot where things can go wrong.
  4. Choose what to focus on. Say it out loud: "I’ll focus on finding and paying for a spot, because that’s where users struggle most."
  5. Sketch the key screens. Rough wireframes of the most important moments. Boxes and labels are enough.
  6. Think about edge cases. No spots available, payment fails, no internet. Showing you thought about these is a big plus.
  7. Wrap up. Summarize what you designed, why, and what you’d explore next with more time and real research.

Throughout all of it: keep talking. Silence is the one thing interviewers can’t evaluate.

When the whiteboard is a browser tab

For remote roles, the whiteboard is usually digital: FigJam, Miro or a similar tool, shared over a video call. It changes a few things.

  • Know the tool before the interview. Fumbling to find the sticky note button eats your time and your confidence. Spend half an hour practicing basic shapes, text, arrows and frames.
  • Ask which tool they’ll use. If they don’t say, ask in advance. Most companies are happy to tell you.
  • Keep it tidy. On a big canvas, things sprawl fast. Use simple sections like "Questions", "User", "Flow" and "Screens" so interviewers can follow you.
  • Narrate even more. Online, interviewers can’t see where you’re looking or what you’re about to do. Say it: "Now I’ll sketch the payment step."
  • Check your setup. Stable internet, working mic, notifications off. A dropped call in minute twenty is a stress you don’t need.

The upside of remote: you can work in a tool you probably use every day anyway, which makes it feel closer to real work than a physical marker ever could.

Mistakes that sink good designers

  • Jumping straight to screens. Drawing a login page in minute one tells interviewers you solve the problem you assumed, not the one in front of you.
  • Designing in silence. Fifteen quiet minutes followed by a finished sketch hides everything they wanted to see.
  • Trying to solve everything. Ten half-done screens look worse than three well-reasoned ones.
  • Ignoring the hints. When an interviewer asks "what about first-time users?", that’s not small talk. It’s a nudge.
  • Defending instead of discussing. Pushback is part of the test. "Good point, here’s how I’d adjust" beats arguing every time.
  • Forgetting the business. A design that users love but the company can’t make money from is only half an answer.
  • No wrap-up. Running out of time without summarizing leaves interviewers to guess what you concluded.

How to practice

The whiteboard challenge is a skill, and like any skill it gets easier with reps.

  • Set a timer and pick a random prompt. A library app, a pet-sitting service, a feature to help people drink more water. Run the full framework in 45 minutes.
  • Practice out loud. Talk through your thinking even when you’re alone. It feels silly at first and becomes natural fast.
  • Record yourself. Watching a replay shows you exactly where you went quiet or lost the thread.
  • Do mock sessions with a friend. Ask them to play a slightly difficult interviewer who pushes back and changes constraints mid-way.
  • Review real products. Pick an app you use and ask: who is it for, what’s the main job, what would I change and why? It builds the same muscle.

After a few sessions, the framework stops being something you remember and becomes the way you think. That’s when the nerves fade.
